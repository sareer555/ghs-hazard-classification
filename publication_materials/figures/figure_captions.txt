FIGURE CAPTIONS
======================================================================

Figure 1. Research workflow. GHS classification annotations were harvested
from PubChem, cleaned and deduplicated, converted into 1218 molecular
descriptors and reduced by variance filtering to the 816 used for modelling,
and split by Bemis-Murcko scaffold so that no chemical skeleton appears in
more than one split. Three algorithms were trained and tuned, evaluated with
threshold calibration and bootstrap confidence intervals, interpreted with
SHAP, and finally validated on Malaysian industrial chemicals before
deployment as a screening tool.

Figure 2. Class distribution of the nine GHS hazard categories in the cleaned
dataset. (a) Number of compounds carrying each pictogram, on a logarithmic
scale. (b) The corresponding imbalance ratio, expressed as negatives per
positive. The two orders of magnitude separating the commonest from the rarest
class is the reason class-imbalance handling was required.

Figure 3. Receiver operating characteristic curves for the best-performing
model (XGBoost) across all nine GHS hazard classes, evaluated on the held-out
scaffold-split test set (n = 24,352 compounds). Each class is shown in its own
panel, ordered by area under the curve from the best-separated class to the
worst; the dashed diagonal marks random guessing and the number of positive
examples is given for each class, since the rarer classes carry the wider
uncertainty.

Figure 4. Precision-recall curves for all three algorithms on two
representative GHS classes: (a) the most abundant class, GHS07, and (b) the
rarest, GHS01. Precision-recall curves are more informative than ROC curves
for heavily imbalanced classes, because the baseline is the class prevalence
rather than 0.5.

Figure 5. SHAP feature-importance summaries for the three GHS classes with the
greatest total feature attribution. Bars show the mean absolute SHAP value of
the five most influential descriptors; colour indicates the direction of the
mean signed effect.

Figure 6. Model comparison heat map showing AUC-ROC for every algorithm and
every GHS hazard class on the scaffold-split test set. Darker cells indicate
better discrimination; each cell is also labelled with its value, so the
comparison does not depend on reading the colour.

Figure 7. Validation on Malaysian industrial chemicals. (a) Per-class
performance on the Malaysian validation set compared with the global scaffold-
split test set. (b) Recall of true hazard labels for each of the four
industrial sectors examined, together with the chemicals implicated in the
March 2019 Sungai Kim Kim incident at Pasir Gudang, Johor.

Figure 8. SHAP waterfall plot explaining an individual prediction. The plot
begins at the model's base value - its average output across the dataset - and
each bar shows how one molecular descriptor moved the prediction towards or
away from the hazard classification, ending at the final predicted
probability. This per-compound explanation is what allows a safety officer to
audit and challenge any individual prediction.

Figure 9. Learning curve: does more training data improve performance within
the 32,000-compound training set actually used? (a) Mean AUC-ROC across the
nine classes as the training subset grows from 1,600 to 32,000 compounds, a
gain of 0.055, against the bootstrap 95 per cent confidence interval on the
final result. (b) The same subsets scored per class; the best and worst
classes are coloured and labelled, the remaining seven shown in grey to keep
the panel legible.

Figure 10. Controlled comparison of training-set size, with the test set,
hyperparameters and feature set held fixed so that quantity of training data
is the only variable. (a) Mean AUC-ROC across the nine classes from 32,000 to
194,658 training compounds, a gain of 0.055 - the memory-limited subset used
for the reported results was not a meaningfully worse starting point than the
full training partition. (b) The same conditions scored per class, best and
worst labelled as in Figure 9.
